### 3. Background and Rationale

#### 3.1 Dry Eye Disease: Definitions and Mechanisms

Dry Eye Disease (DED) is a multifactorial condition characterised by loss of tear film homeostasis, resulting in discomfort, visual disturbances, and tear film instability (Wolffsohn et al. 2025; Craig et al. 2017). The TFOS DEWS II report defines DED as "a multifactorial disease of the ocular surface characterised by a loss of homeostasis of the tear film, and accompanied by ocular symptoms, in which tear film instability and hyperosmolarity, ocular surface inflammation and damage, and neurosensory abnormalities play etiological roles" (Craig et al. 2017). The updated TFOS DEWS III report (Wolffsohn et al. 2025) refines this definition, emphasising the interplay between aqueous deficiency, evaporative loss, and surface damage.

DED affects an estimated 5–50% of the global population depending on diagnostic criteria, geography, and study population (Stapleton et al. 2017). A recent systematic review and meta-analysis estimates a global pooled prevalence of 34.6% (Xiao et al. 2025). Prevalence is higher in females, older adults, and populations in arid or high-pollution environments (Stapleton et al. 2017). In Asia, prevalence rates of 20–30% are reported in urban populations (Stapleton et al. 2017).

The pathological mechanism linking DED to visual impairment operates through tear film instability, which degrades optical quality by increasing higher-order aberrations (HOAs) and reducing contrast sensitivity (Koh 2016; Koh et al. 2002). Tear film break-up — whether measured as Tear Film Break-Up Time (TBUT) or Non-Invasive Break-Up Time (NIBUT) — directly predicts the magnitude of wavefront error and the degree of visual degradation (Koh et al. 2002). In clinical studies, individuals with DED demonstrate significantly elevated HOAs compared to controls, and these aberrations fluctuate with tear film dynamics throughout the blink cycle (Koh 2016). DED also degrades optical quality through surface irregularities that compromise the precorneal tear film as the eye's most anterior refractive surface (Herbaut et al. 2018).

#### 3.2 Night Driving: Environmental and Visual Demands

Night driving represents a mesopic visual environment where both rod and cone photoreceptors are active (Artal 2014). Pupil dilation under low-light conditions amplifies the impact of HOAs on retinal image quality (Wood 2020). Simultaneously, glare from oncoming headlights creates disability glare, which reduces contrast sensitivity and increases the time required for visual recovery (Puell et al. 2004; Maniglia et al. 2018).

Road crash fatality rates are approximately three times higher during night driving than daytime driving (Varghese & Shankar 2007). Pedestrians are approximately seven times more likely to be killed in nighttime crashes compared to daytime crashes, a finding demonstrated through analysis of ambient light levels and fatal crash patterns (Sullivan & Flannagan 2002). Additionally, improved roadway lighting has been shown to reduce pedestrian crash risk, further confirming the role of visibility in night-driving safety (Sullivan & Flannagan 2007). The visual demands of night driving include: (1) detection of low-contrast objects against a dark background; (2) recognition of road markings and signs under mesopic illumination; (3) rapid adaptation to glare from oncoming headlights; (4) maintenance of lane position using peripheral visual cues; and (5) judgment of speed and distance under degraded visibility (Owsley & McGwin 2010; Wood 2020). Any condition that impairs contrast sensitivity, increases glare sensitivity, or degrades optical quality will disproportionately affect night-driving performance.

#### 3.3 The Intersection: DED and Night-Driving Difficulty

DED is uniquely positioned to impair night-driving performance through multiple converging mechanisms:

1. Tear film instability increases HOAs, degrading mesopic vision (Koh 2016; Koh et al. 2002)
2. Reduced contrast sensitivity impairs detection of low-contrast objects (Deschamps et al. 2013)
3. Glare sensitivity amplifies the visual impact of oncoming headlights (Puell et al. 2004; Maniglia et al. 2018)
4. Ocular surface discomfort creates distraction and reduces attention (Goto et al. 2002)

Despite these plausible mechanisms, the evidence linking DED/dry-eye symptoms specifically to night-driving difficulty remains fragmented across optometry, ophthalmology, transportation science, and public health literature.

#### 3.4 Existing Evidence

The following evidence clusters have been identified through preliminary scoping:

1. **DED and driving performance:** Deschamps et al. (2013) demonstrated that DED patients showed impaired driving simulator performance. Tong et al. (2010) reported associations between DED symptoms and self-reported driving difficulty. Wang et al. (2017) found that drivers with DED had higher frequencies of unsafe driving habits.

2. **Tear film metrics and night driving:** Woi et al. (2025a) demonstrated that both NIBUT (objective tear film stability) and OSDI (dry-eye symptoms) significantly predict self-reported night-driving difficulty in Malaysian adults, explaining 41.9% of variance. NIBUT and OSDI were weakly correlated (r = −0.196), suggesting they capture distinct aspects of DED. Spearman's correlations showed moderate negative associations between NIBUT (r = −0.327) and VND-Q scores, and between OSDI (r = −0.538) and VND-Q scores.

3. **Self-report instruments:** The Vision and Night Driving Questionnaire (VND-Q) was developed by Kimlin et al. (2016). Woi et al. (2025b) validated the Malay version (VND-Q-M), demonstrating a two-factor structure (visibility, distance judgment) with CVI of 0.98 and Cronbach's α of 0.84. Wan Din (2026) used the VND-Q-M to show that dry-eye symptom severity (OSDI) predicts night-driving difficulty (adjusted R² = .225, p < .001) in 373 Malaysian drivers aged 18–40 with self-reported dry-eye symptoms. Spearman's correlation showed a moderate positive association between OSDI and VND-Q-M scores (r = .489, p < .001).

4. **Vision impairment and driving:** Owsley and McGwin (2010) reviewed vision and driving, including age-related visual impairments. Existing evidence covers multiple conditions (cataract, glaucoma, AMD, field loss) but DED-specific evidence is limited.

**No existing review has specifically mapped the evidence on DED/dry-eye symptoms and night-driving difficulty.** The proposed scoping review fills this gap.

#### 3.5 Relationship to the Primary Thesis and Published Papers

This review builds upon the thesis "Elucidating the Association Between Dry Eye Symptoms, Self-Esteem and Night Driving Difficulty in Adult Drivers" (Wan Din 2026), its companion draft manuscript (WPJ, unpublished), and two associated publications (Woi et al. 2025a; Woi et al. 2025b) by:

- Positioning these findings within the broader international evidence base
- Mapping all available evidence on DED/dry-eye symptoms and night driving
- Identifying where these studies fill geographic (Malaysian population) and methodological gaps
- Identifying where clinical validation studies are needed

**Description of the original study:** The thesis (Wan Din 2026) and its companion draft manuscript (WPJ) report the same cross-sectional survey study of 373 licensed Malaysian drivers aged 18–40 who drove at night at least once per week. Both reports assess the same three self-reported constructs using validated Malay-language questionnaires: dry-eye symptom severity (OSDI-M), vision-related night-driving difficulty (VND-Q-M), and global self-esteem (RSES-M). No clinical examination was performed. The dry-eye component was based on self-reported symptoms, not clinical diagnosis. Participants with a known clinical diagnosis of dry eye disease were excluded. Both reports found that dry-eye symptom severity independently predicted self-reported night-driving difficulty (adjusted R² = .225, p < .001), while self-esteem (p = .256) and gender (p = .787) were not significant predictors. The draft manuscript is designated as the primary report for numerical synthesis because it is the version intended for peer-reviewed publication and focuses more directly on the DED-night-driving relationship. The thesis is recorded as a companion report and is not counted as independent evidence.

**Important distinction regarding study populations:**
- The thesis and draft manuscript studied participants with self-reported dry-eye symptoms assessed via the OSDI questionnaire, with clinically diagnosed DED excluded.
- Publication 2 (Woi et al. 2025a) included participants with dry-eye symptoms and used NIBUT (objective tear film stability measure) alongside OSDI. NIBUT measures tear film break-up time — a physical property of the tear film — not a clinical DED diagnosis. No participant in Publication 2 was clinically diagnosed with DED.
- The Scoping Review maps ALL evidence on DED/dry-eye symptoms and night driving, including studies with diagnosed DED, undiagnosed symptoms, and mixed populations. The review scope is deliberately broader than the thesis population to capture the full evidence landscape.

#### 3.6 Why a Scoping Review Is Appropriate

A scoping review is the most appropriate methodology because:

1. The research question is broad — mapping the evidence landscape, not estimating a single pooled effect
2. The evidence is heterogeneous — spanning self-report surveys, clinical studies, simulator studies, on-road tests, and naturalistic driving studies
3. The review aims to identify gaps, inconsistencies, and future research priorities
4. No existing review addresses this specific intersection
5. The Arksey and O'Malley (2005) framework, refined by Levac et al. (2010), and reported per PRISMA-ScR (Tricco et al. 2018), provides a rigorous, transparent, and repeatable methodology

### 4. Objectives

#### 4.1 Primary Objective

To map the available evidence on the relationship between DED/dry-eye symptoms and night-driving difficulty among drivers.

#### 4.2 Secondary Objectives

1. To identify and categorise the study designs, populations, settings, and geographic regions represented in the evidence base
2. To map the assessment instruments used for both dry-eye symptoms and night-driving difficulty
3. To identify whether studies use self-report, clinical, or combined measures of DED/dry-eye symptoms
4. To identify whether studies use self-report, simulator, on-road, or combined measures of night-driving difficulty
5. To map intermediate visual function measures (contrast sensitivity, HOAs, glare, tear film stability) reported in studies examining DED and driving
6. To characterise the methodological quality distribution of included studies (descriptive, not exclusionary)
7. To identify evidence gaps and priorities for future research

---

### 5. Review Questions

#### 5.1 Primary Review Question

**What is the available evidence on the relationship between DED/dry-eye symptoms and night-driving difficulty among drivers?**

#### 5.2 Secondary Questions

1. What study designs, populations, and settings are represented in this evidence base?
2. What instruments and methods are used to assess dry-eye symptoms and night-driving difficulty?
3. What associations between DED/dry-eye symptoms and night-driving difficulty have been reported?
4. What intermediate visual function factors (tear film stability, HOAs, contrast sensitivity, glare) are investigated in relation to DED and driving?
5. What is the geographic distribution of the evidence?
6. What evidence gaps exist in the current literature?

**Population boundary notes:**
- "Licensed drivers" includes any current driving licence holder, regardless of vehicle type
- "Drive at night or in low-light conditions" includes any frequency of night driving
- No age restriction applied (the thesis studied 18–40; the review maps all ages)
- No geographic restriction applied (the thesis was Malaysia-specific; the review is global)

**Concept boundary notes:**
- DED/dry-eye symptoms includes diagnosed DED, undiagnosed dry-eye symptoms, subclinical dry-eye, and symptom-only assessment
- Night-driving difficulty includes self-report, simulator, on-road, naturalistic, and crash-data measures
- Intermediate visual function measures (contrast sensitivity, HOAs, glare, tear film stability) are included when studied in the context of DED and driving
- Self-esteem is NOT included as a review concept — it is thesis-specific and not a core element of the DED-night-driving intersection
- The review is not restricted to studies that directly test an association between DED and night-driving difficulty; it maps how DED/dry-eye symptoms are studied in drivers, how night-driving difficulty is defined/measured, and how night/low-light visual performance is assessed

#### 8.2 Detailed Inclusion Criteria

**Population:**
- Licensed drivers of any age
- Driving any motor vehicle (car, motorcycle, truck, bus, or other)
- Driving at night or in low-light conditions
- Any geographic setting
- Any employment or student status
- Studies reporting driver-specific data within mixed populations (drivers + non-drivers)

**Concept — DED/Dry-Eye:**
- Dry Eye Disease (DED) — any diagnostic criteria (TFOS DEWS II, TFOS DEWS III, ADES, clinical diagnosis, or study-specific definition)
- Dry-eye symptoms — measured by any validated or custom questionnaire (OSDI, SPEED, DEQ, CLDEQ, IDEEL, NEI-VFQ, VND-Q, or custom instruments)
- Subclinical dry-eye symptoms
- Tear film instability as a proxy for DED (TBUT, NIBUT, tear film osmolarity)
- Visual function measures in the context of DED and driving: contrast sensitivity, HOAs, glare sensitivity, mesopic visual acuity, scotopic visual acuity

**Concept — Night-Driving:**
- Night-driving difficulty — measured by any method: self-report questionnaires (VND-Q, VND-Q-M, NDQ, LLQ, custom), driving simulators, on-road tests, naturalistic driving data, crash data, near-miss data

#### 8.3 Detailed Exclusion Criteria

- Studies with no measure of dry-eye symptoms or DED (even if DED is mentioned in the discussion or background)
- Studies with no measure of night-driving difficulty or low-light driving performance
- Studies on general driving performance without night/low-light conditions
- Studies on other ocular diseases without a DED/dry-eye component (e.g., cataract alone, glaucoma alone, AMD alone — unless DED is also assessed and data are reported separately)
- Non-human studies (in vitro, animal models)
- Conference abstracts failing any of the four PC-09 minima (no N, no DED measure, no night-driving outcome, or no design — e.g., title-only, protocol-only, no results) → exclude under E5
- Editorials, commentaries, letters without original data
- Systematic reviews and scoping reviews (but reference lists will be hand-searched)

#### 9.3 Double-Counting Prevention Rule

**Rule:** Where multiple reports originate from the same study or dataset, only one primary report is included in the numerical evidence synthesis. Companion reports are recorded in the charting form but flagged as "companion to [primary report]" and not counted as independent evidence.

**Primary report selection criteria (in order of priority):**
1. Intended for peer-reviewed publication
2. Most focused relevance to the DED-night-driving relationship
3. Most complete dataset
4. Most recent publication date

**Applied to this review:**
- Draft Manuscript (WPJ) = primary report (intended for publication; focused on DED-night-driving)
- Wan Din (2026) thesis = companion report (same dataset; not counted as independent evidence)

### 12. Study Selection

#### 12.1 Selection Process

**Phase 1: Title and Abstract Screening**
- Two independent reviewers screen all titles and abstracts against eligibility criteria
- Screening is performed using Rayyan (rayyan.ai) or Covidence
- Each record receives one of three decisions: INCLUDE, EXCLUDE, or UNCERTAIN
- Disagreements resolved by discussion or third reviewer
- Inter-rater reliability calculated using Cohen's kappa on a 10% random sample before full screening begins (calibration gate)
- Final inter-rater reliability for the entirety of title/abstract screening calculated and reported using Cohen's kappa (PC-13)

**Phase 2: Full-Text Screening**
- Full texts of all INCLUDE and UNCERTAIN records retrieved
- Two independent reviewers assess full texts against eligibility criteria
- Each record receives a final INCLUDE or EXCLUDE decision
- Reasons for exclusion documented for all excluded records
- Disagreements resolved by discussion or third reviewer

**Phase 3: Grey-Literature Screening**
- Grey-literature records are screened using the same two-phase process
- No preferential treatment given to any source type
- Theses/dissertations and unpublished manuscripts are screened with identical criteria as journal articles

#### 12.2 Exclusion Reasons (Full-Text)

| Code | Reason |
|---|---|
| E1 | No DED/dry-eye measure |
| E2 | No night-driving or low-light driving measure |
| E3 | Wrong population (non-drivers only) |
| E4 | Wrong study design (review, editorial, commentary, case report) |
| E5 | Conference abstract without sufficient data |
| E6 | Duplicate publication — identical or near-identical content (e.g., verbatim thesis-chapter article, same article in two journals); keep the most authoritative version (see §17.1–17.2) |
| E7 | Companion report — same dataset but different analysis/framing as an included primary report; recorded as companion, not counted in numerical synthesis (see §17.1, §17.3) |
| E8 | Full text not retrievable |
| E9 | Other (specify) |

**E6 vs E7 disambiguation rule (CR-06 audit amendment 2026-09-03):** identical content → E6 (exclude duplicate); same data with different analysis → E7 (companion). Apply §17.1 definitions; borderline cases go to the arbiter.

#### 12.3 Documentation

- PRISMA-ScR flow diagram will document the selection process at each stage
- Inter-rater reliability (Cohen's kappa) will be reported for title/abstract screening: calibration kappa (10% sample) plus final kappa for the full screening phase (PC-13)

### 14. Data Synthesis and Presentation

#### 14.1 Synthesis Approach

1. **Descriptive numerical summary:** Frequency counts of studies by design, population, setting, geography, instruments, and assessment types. Presented as tables and bar charts.

2. **Evidence mapping:** Cross-tabulation of studies across key dimensions (e.g., study design × DED assessment type; night-driving measure × geographic region). Presented as frequency tables.

3. **Thematic synthesis:** Grouping studies by approach (self-report only, clinical only, combined; simulator, on-road, naturalistic). Narrative summary of each theme.

4. **Gap analysis:** Identification of cells in the evidence map where no or thin evidence exists. Presented as a gap matrix.

5. **Quality characterisation:** Description of the distribution of quality characteristics across included studies (descriptive, not exclusionary).

6. **Source-overlap audit:** Transparent reporting of which reports are companion publications from the same study/dataset, and how they are represented in the evidence map.

**No meta-analysis is planned.** The evidence is expected to be too heterogeneous for quantitative pooling, and the review objective is mapping, not estimation.

### 17. Duplicate / Companion-Report Handling

#### 17.1 Definitions

| Term | Definition | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Duplicate** | Two or more reports that are identical or near-identical in content (e.g., same article published in two journals, or a published article that is a verbatim copy of a thesis chapter) | Keep the most complete/authoritative version; exclude the other(s) |
| **Companion report** | Two or more reports that share the same underlying dataset or study but present different analyses, outcomes, or theoretical framings | Record both; include only one as primary for numerical synthesis; flag the other(s) as companion(s) |
| **Primary report** | The companion report that provides the most complete, relevant, and authoritative account of the study for this review's purposes | Used in numerical synthesis and evidence mapping |
| **Companion (flagged)** | Additional reports from the same study, recorded for transparency but not counted as independent evidence | Recorded in charting form; not counted in study-level synthesis |

#### 17.2 Duplicate Detection

**Detection methods:**
1. Mendeley automatic duplicate detection (identical metadata)
2. Manual review of author lists, sample sizes, study dates, and geographic locations
3. Cross-referencing of reference lists
4. Forward citation searching (Web of Science)

**Resolution:**
- Where two reports are identical (e.g., thesis chapter published as journal article), keep the peer-reviewed version and exclude the thesis
- Where a thesis contains additional data not in the journal article, the thesis is retained as a companion report

#### 17.5 Self-Review Safeguards

Because the lead reviewer is also an author of the thesis, the draft manuscript, and co-author of Woi et al. 2025a and Woi et al. 2025b, the following safeguards apply:

1. **Eligibility criteria applied identically:** The thesis, draft manuscript, and Woi papers are screened using the exact same criteria as all other sources. No preferential treatment.
2. **Second-reviewer verification:** The second reviewer independently screens the thesis, draft manuscript, and Woi papers against eligibility criteria and verifies all charting decisions on these reports

### 20. Anticipated Limitations and Challenges

| Challenge | Description | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Terminological variation | DED referred to by multiple names | Comprehensive search strategy with multiple synonyms |
| Variation in DED definitions | TFOS DEWS II, TFOS DEWS III, ADES, clinical, symptom-based | Data charting captures definition; stratification by definition type |
| Variation in night-driving measures | Self-report, simulator, on-road, naturalistic | Data charting captures measure type; stratification by measure type |
| Heterogeneity of evidence | Clinical studies, surveys, simulator studies, on-road tests | Scoping review designed for heterogeneous evidence; synthesis is descriptive |
| Geographic bias | Evidence may be concentrated in high-income countries | Data charting captures geography; gap analysis identifies underrepresented regions |
| Language restriction | English and Malay only | Acknowledged as limitation (justification: §8.1 language note); reference lists of non-English studies hand-searched |
| Full-text retrieval | Some articles may not be accessible | Interlibrary loan; author contact; institutional access |
| Inter-rater reliability | Disagreements during screening/charting | Calibration phases; clear definitions; arbitration process |
| Grey-literature retrieval | Theses and unpublished manuscripts may be difficult to locate | Multiple grey-literature databases; author contact; institutional repositories |
| Companion-report complexity | Thesis, draft manuscript, and published papers may overlap | A priori relationship mapping (Section 9.2); primary-report selection criteria (Section 17.4); second-reviewer verification |
| VisionCite access | VisionCite is a paid subscription database; UKM PTSL does not currently subscribe | Attempt via ILL/direct request in Days 1–5 verification window (§10.1.1). If inaccessible, record explicitly as not-searched; fallback is documented MEDLINE + Embase + CINAHL overlap (not a full replacement for trade/association journals) — see PC-06 |
| Institutional database access | CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science, TRID access through UKM PTSL not yet confirmed | Formal verification in Days 1–5 (§10.1.1, PC-07): record accessible/inaccessible + reason per manifest; PubMed/MEDLINE is freely accessible |
| Search update timing | Formal strategy in §11.8 (PC-15): monthly re-runs Months 1–4 + final update ≤3 months pre-submission; primary vs update yields reported separately in PRISMA flow | See §11.8; manifests carry Review Stage (Primary/Update) |
| Self-review bias | Lead reviewer is author of thesis and draft manuscript | Second-reviewer independent screening; transparent reporting; no preferential treatment |
